# CLEARANCE

**FILM · Action disaster thriller · 2h 07m · PG-13**

**After a massive earthquake cripples Los Angeles’ eastern power grid, a heavy-haul foreman has one night to move a 680-ton replacement transformer from the Port to a mountain substation across forty-one miles of roads that no longer match the route.**

## THE PITCH

Rae Voss moves things that roads were not built to move.

Industrial boilers. Refinery vessels. Turbines the size of houses. Entire bridge sections. The work happens mostly between midnight and dawn, with police closing intersections, utility crews lifting wires, somebody walking ahead with a measuring pole, and Rae standing in the street deciding whether several hundred tons of machinery actually fit through the space everyone else has already agreed they do.

At 2:14 on a Thursday morning, Rae is finishing a routine job near the Port of Los Angeles when the earthquake hits.

By 3:00, large parts of the city are dark. By 3:40, grid coordinator Lena Cho is standing beside a 680-ton autotransformer that arrived at the port three days earlier and was supposed to leave by rail the following week.

The rail connection is gone. Two major substations inland are down.

There is another way to Mesa Vista: forty-one miles of surface streets, industrial corridors, freeway frontage roads, bridges, grades, utility crossings, and turns that were surveyed for absolutely nothing like this.

Lena unfolds the route map across the hood of a truck. Two crossings on the highlighted line have already been closed.

“How long?”

Lena tells her.

Rae laughs once.

Then she starts calling people.

## THE CHARACTERS

**Rae Voss** walks every difficult move before the transporter does. She checks the curb, crown of the road, gutter, wires, poles, parked cars, and anything somebody has described as probably fine.

If a drawing says fourteen inches and her tape says eleven, the drawing loses. She carries sidewalk chalk in one pocket because she would rather put a number on pavement herself than remember what somebody told her over a radio.

Rae almost never says something will fit.

She says how much room it has.

**Niko Reyes** operates the modular transporter, which means his workday is spent with a control pendant in his hands telling dozens of independently steerable axle lines where to point.

He can move a platform nearly a city block long sideways, diagonally, or almost in place. Before Rae asks for anything especially ugly, Niko tends to look at the load, look at the available road, and ask whether everyone is certain.

Rae usually says no.

**DeShawn Booker** runs the utility-clearance crew. Dee knows which traffic signals can swing away, which wires can be raised, which poles can tolerate a temporary change in tension, and which municipal drawings were apparently completed by someone who never visited the street.

He and Rae have worked together long enough that she can point at an intersection and say, “I hate that,” and Dee will begin looking upward.

**Lena Cho** carries two radios because the power system and the convoy are using different channels, and she has developed the habit of holding one against each side of her vest while somebody else talks.

Every few miles, utility control asks for an arrival time. Lena writes the question down anyway, even after Rae stops giving her one.

## THE CAST

**Mackenzie Davis is Rae Voss**, and the best thing she does with the role is make competence look active rather than serene. Rae is constantly looking somewhere: pavement, wheel lines, the underside of an overpass, the crew member who has stopped talking. Davis gives her the physical impatience of somebody whose brain has reached the problem before the rest of the intersection has.

**Manny Jacinto is Niko Reyes**, spending much of the movie twelve feet below everybody else beside an enormous field of tires, glancing between Rae and the load before deciding which one currently looks less alarming. He gets some of the movie’s funniest reactions without turning Niko into comic relief.

**Brian Tyree Henry is DeShawn Booker**, who can communicate an impressive range of objections by slowly taking off his safety glasses. Dee and Rae do not need a friendship speech. Half their relationship is contained in how quickly each notices when the other has gone quiet.

**Hong Chau is Lena Cho**, usually holding a radio microphone just below her mouth even when nobody is speaking. Some of her best moments are tiny calculations happening behind her eyes while somebody gives her an answer that contains absolutely none of the precision she requested.

## THE MOVIE

Before the earthquake, Rae’s crew is moving a refinery vessel through Wilmington at two in the morning, a huge white cylinder riding sideways across a platform with more tires than most parking lots. The planned turn requires the rear axle lines to clear a traffic-signal pole by fourteen inches while the front swings past a closed pharmacy.

Then they find a delivery van parked exactly where the rear of the load needs to go.

Rae walks the intersection twice. Niko rotates the axle lines. Dee gets the signal arm swung over the sidewalk, and the vessel begins sliding diagonally through the crossing at walking speed.

For about thirty seconds, the geometry looks absurd. The rear clears the van by four inches. The front misses the pharmacy awning by six.

Afterward, the city inspector measures a tire mark on the curb while half the crew eats breakfast burritos off the hood of a pickup.

Four hours later, 680 tons of steel, copper, oil, and ceramic begin rolling out of the port toward Mesa Vista.

The convoy has bucket trucks ahead, police vehicles at intersections, mechanics behind, utility crews leapfrogging forward, and scouts finding out one road at a time whether the earthquake has left them anything usable. An aftershock drops masonry across one route. A water-main break takes another. Inspectors close a freeway crossing after finding a support column shifted off its bearing.

Rae keeps crossing streets off Lena’s paper map.

The first real problem arrives beneath a damaged rail bridge.

The marked clearance was supposed to work. Dee measures it again and gets eight inches less than they need.

Lena starts looking for another route.

Rae asks how much asphalt is above the old concrete base.

Nobody knows.

Twenty minutes later, two municipal excavators are tearing into the intersection.

They strip the roadway down in layers while Niko lowers the transporter suspension. Under the first few inches of asphalt they uncover faded lane markings from some previous version of the street, followed by older concrete nobody knew was still there.

Dee walks the opening with his measuring pole.

Still too high.

The excavators take another layer.

This time Dee looks at Rae and holds up three fingers.

The transformer enters the underpass with less than three inches above its highest bushing. Loose grit falls from the bridge onto the casing while Niko moves slowly enough that one of the police officers walks beside the same wheel for the entire crossing.

On the far side, Rae looks back at the trench they have cut through a public road.

“Put it back.”

The excavator operator stares at her.

“Eventually.”

A few miles later, the city stops giving Rae problems she can measure.

Their replacement route crosses a concrete culvert over a drainage channel. The road surface looks intact. The original drawings show enough capacity. An engineer on the phone has photographs from one side and no way to inspect the underside before they arrive.

Niko asks for the number.

Rae does not have one.

Behind them, another crossing has just been closed.

She walks halfway across the culvert, kneels beside a crack running from the curb toward the centerline, and puts the chalk back in her pocket without writing anything.

“Bring me the front twelve axles.”

Niko looks at her.

“Just twelve?”

“Twelve.”

The first section of the transporter creeps onto the concrete without the transformer above it. Nothing moves.

Rae sends another twelve.

Still nothing.

Then she brings the load forward.

There is a sound from beneath the road that is not especially loud.

Rae's hand goes up immediately.

One edge of the culvert has dropped several inches.

The convoy reverses at less than walking speed and spends the next forty minutes backing a city-block-long transporter through two turns they already celebrated clearing.

Nobody says anything to Rae about the lost time.

Niko does eventually hand her a fresh piece of sidewalk chalk.

After that, the crew keeps changing the city wherever the city still lets them.

Curbs disappear beneath steel plate. Signal heads rotate over sidewalks. Tow trucks drag abandoned cars out of turning radii. At one six-lane intersection, Niko turns the axle groups in different directions and the transformer slides sideways across the road while firefighters watch from the median.

My favorite small thing is how quickly everybody stops finding any of this remarkable. Hours into the move, the transformer passes a gas station and the cashier walks outside with his phone like somebody told him there was an interesting dog in the parking lot.

The roads are beginning to climb.

## THE SCENES

### The balcony

Near Highland Park, the route squeezes between an apartment building and a row of mature street trees the city will not remove unless there is no other option.

Dee takes every branch he can. Residents move two parked cars. Niko folds the transporter’s mirrors inward even though they barely matter at this scale.

The transformer begins moving through at less than one mile per hour.

People come out onto their balconies.

Nobody in the convoy acknowledges them. Everyone is measuring.

On the third floor, a little boy reaches through the railing and waves as the top of the transformer passes almost level with him.

Niko looks up and waves back with the control pendant.

A woman two balconies down begins filming him instead of the transformer.

Then Rae tells him he has nine inches on the right, and Niko is looking at tires again.

### Under the load

Later, one hydraulic circuit starts overheating.

The transporter stops diagonally across an intersection while Niko crawls underneath with a mechanic. He lies on flattened cardboard beside the failing line while Rae reads pressure values off his monitor and a police officer keeps asking whether they can open one lane.

The streetlights begin moving.

For a second, nobody reacts because the entire night has been full of trucks, generators, cranes, and things vibrating that normally do not.

Then the pavement rolls.

The transporter starts drifting toward the low side of the intersection with Niko still underneath it.

Rae grabs the pendant.

She normally calls the movement. Niko normally makes it happen.

Now she has ninety-six axle lines, a control screen she knows but does not operate, and several hundred tons beginning to move in the wrong direction.

Dee and two mechanics get hold of Niko’s shoulders.

Rae steers.

The transporter moves less than two feet before it settles.

Niko comes out without his hard hat.

Nobody knows where it went.

### The wire

Much later, they reach a residential stretch where one distribution line is sagging lower than the utility map says it should.

Dee’s clearance truck is two intersections ahead dealing with another crossing. Rae can see the line. She cannot tell whether it is the wire itself hanging low or the pole leaning toward the street.

She calls Dee.

No answer.

Lena looks at the time.

Rae looks at the transformer.

Then a porch light comes on in the house beside them.

An older man steps outside in slippers, looks at the convoy occupying his entire street, and asks whether they need him to move his truck.

Rae asks how long the pole has been leaning.

He looks up.

“What pole?”

She turns around.

The wooden utility pole on the opposite side of the street is moving almost imperceptibly every time the wind catches the line.

Rae backs the convoy half a block.

Thirty seconds later, the top of the pole gives way and lands across the road where the transformer would have been.

The man in slippers looks at Rae.

“Truck okay?”

Rae looks at his pickup.

“Truck’s great.”

## THE FINISH

The last road into Mesa Vista climbs beside a concrete flood-control channel before crossing a narrow bridge onto the substation property.

The bridge is still standing.

The approach is not.

A section of roadway has dropped into the channel, leaving a gap too wide to plate and too unstable to rebuild before morning. Behind the convoy, the transformer fills the road from curb to curb. Ahead of it, the substation is close enough that the crew can see emergency work lights moving behind the fence.

Two crawler cranes from a nearby construction site arrive shortly before dawn, one on each side of the break. They were never part of the route. Neither was the plan Rae draws in chalk across the hood of Dee’s truck.

The transporter inches forward until its leading axle lines are almost at the missing pavement. Crews attach lifting frames to the transformer while Niko crawls beneath the platform one final time to release the load points. Dee’s people clear everyone out of the channel. Lena’s radio keeps talking from the passenger seat of an empty truck.

The cranes take tension together.

Steel cables straighten above the transformer. The suspension rises as the weight begins leaving the transporter, one axle line after another, until daylight appears beneath the first row of tires.

Then the entire transformer lifts clear.

For the first time since the port, 680 tons of steel is hanging over nothing.
