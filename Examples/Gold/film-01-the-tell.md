# THE TELL

**FILM · Sports comedy-drama · 1h 54m · PG-13**

**A washed-up professional poker player takes over a winless girls’ high-school basketball team and discovers that reading people under pressure may be the one skill he learned at a card table that is actually useful somewhere else.**

## THE PITCH

Frank Mercer takes the job for the least inspirational reason available: it pays $3,200, lasts eleven weeks, and nobody else in town is willing to coach a girls’ basketball team that has already lost eight straight games.

He is fifty-six and newly back in the Michigan town he spent most of his adult life avoiding, living out of two suitcases in the house his mother left behind. For nearly twenty years, Frank made his money playing poker wherever somebody would put up a table and let him sit down. He was good enough to build a life around it, then bad enough at almost everything surrounding the game to eventually lose most of that life anyway.

The job comes from Danny Rusk, his oldest friend and now the athletic director at Lakewood High, who needs an adult with a clean background check and approximately two months of free time. Frank satisfies both requirements. Basketball knowledge, Danny assures him, would be nice but has recently become optional.

By the end of Frank’s first practice, the team would prefer they had simply forfeited the season. Frank may know almost nothing about basketball, but he knows an uncomfortable amount about people, and this team is giving away tells everywhere.

## THE CHARACTERS

**Frank Mercer** has spent most of his adult life noticing things other people would rather keep hidden. A bad swallow. A hand that suddenly stops moving. Somebody who becomes much funnier when they are nervous.

Danny asks whether Frank wants coffee and gets told that he has been sleeping in his office again. A player says her ankle hurts and Frank asks why it only started hurting after she missed two shots. Ordinary conversations with him have an irritating tendency to end somewhere you never agreed to go.

**Mara Bell** teaches algebra at Lakewood, played Division III basketball, and has been the girls’ assistant coach for three years. She knows every set they run, every opponent in the conference, which player needs to be yelled at, and which one will cry if you so much as change your tone.

Frank spends his first month asking Mara what various basketball terms mean in the middle of practice. She answers because somebody has to. By January, they can have an entire argument from opposite ends of the gym without either one raising their voice.

**Tasha Reed** is Lakewood’s senior point guard and easily its best player. She gets to practice early enough to shoot before anyone asks her to, and after bad calls she is usually the one handing the ball calmly back to the official before somebody else on the team earns a technical.

She has also spent four years hearing that she could play in college if she became more aggressive, more vocal, more confident, more consistent, and several other things adults apparently believe can be switched on independently. Her father, Marcus, owns two car dealerships outside town, pays for the team’s warmups, never misses a game, and usually has an opinion ready before Tasha has changed out of her uniform.

**Erin Caldwell**, Lakewood’s six-foot senior center, responds to pressure by becoming medically fascinating. Her ankle starts hurting in close games. Her shoulder acts up before free throws. After missing two layups in the same quarter, she once develops what she calls “a weird rib thing.”

Frank likes her immediately.

## THE CAST

**Walton Goggins is Frank Mercer**, and about five minutes in the whole thing makes sense. Frank’s little half-smile can look charming from across the room and vaguely dangerous once you realize he has already figured out what somebody is trying to hide. Goggins also lets the quiet stretches sit there without rushing to explain them.

**Molly Gordon is Mara Bell**, who spends a surprising amount of the movie looking at Frank as though she is deciding whether correcting him is worth the energy. It usually is. Their scenes get funnier once Mara stops being shocked by him and starts beating him to the observation.

**Whitney Peak is Tasha Reed**, playing her with the kind of composure adults tend to mistake for confidence. Some of her best moments happen after somebody asks whether she is okay and she gives an answer just convincing enough to end the conversation.

**Shea Whigham is Danny Rusk**, Frank’s oldest friend and the one person in town immune to his stories. Danny has a wonderful habit of waiting through the entire polished version of an explanation before asking, “What actually happened?”

## THE MOVIE

Lakewood High in January is gray snow piled against gray curbs, salted hallways, fluorescent locker rooms, and a gym that somehow feels freezing until about two hundred people get inside it. Girls’ varsity follows the boys’ JV most nights, so the opening quarter is usually played in front of parents, younger siblings, and whoever has decided the concession-stand nachos count as dinner.

Frank arrives in the middle of all this like someone who wandered into the wrong building and decided not to admit it. He does not own a whistle, has learned maybe half the plays, and by his third practice has dragged an old leather recliner out of the teachers’ lounge and planted it beside the bench.

“If I’m going to watch us turn the ball over thirty times, I’m going to be comfortable.”

By January, nobody even looks at the recliner anymore.

The girls hate Frank at first, particularly once he begins noticing things. One guard looks toward Mara before forcing passes because she has forgotten what comes next. Erin’s limp becomes more convincing as the score gets closer. During film sessions, Frank cares less about where the ball went than what a player was doing five seconds before it got there.

Mara starts turning some of those observations into basketball. Frank notices an opposing center touching the waistband of her shorts whenever she is exhausted, so Mara attacks her in transition. He points out that one of Lakewood’s guards speeds up after mistakes, and Mara changes who brings the ball up after turnovers. Neither of them would describe the arrangement as collaboration.

Lakewood starts winning just enough for the whole experiment to become interesting. They remain fully capable of throwing the ball directly to the other team, then arguing about whose fault it was while the other team is still scoring, but the gym begins filling earlier. Students start sitting behind the bench. A parent takes a picture of Frank eating peanut M&M’s during a timeout, and by Monday morning Danny has received the photograph from three different people.

The trouble comes during a close road game when Marcus arrives midway through the second quarter. Over the next few minutes, Tasha forces a drive into two defenders, launches a three early in the shot clock, and waves off one of Mara’s calls.

Frank waits through one more possession before calling timeout. The girls gather around Mara’s clipboard while Frank looks directly at Tasha.

“Your dad got here six minutes ago.”

Tasha says nothing.

“You haven’t run the offense once since.”

The gym is loud enough that the players on the outside of the huddle lean closer. Tasha looks at Frank for a long second, and whatever she is thinking does not make it to her face.

The horn sounds.

## THE SCENES

### “She’s done.”

Later in the season, Lakewood gets another shot at the best team in the conference, a school that embarrassed them the first time around.

With ninety seconds left, Lakewood is down three. Mara starts drawing something during the timeout, but Frank is staring at the other bench. Their star guard has played almost the entire game and has started rubbing the inside of her left knee every time the whistle blows.

Frank leans toward Mara.

“She’s done.”

Mara looks over.

“She’s scored twenty-six.”

“I didn’t say she’s bad. I said she’s done.”

Mara watches the girl for another second, turns back to the clipboard, and erases what she just drew.

### The parent meeting

After Frank benches a starter for ignoring Mara three possessions in a row, somebody requests a parent meeting.

About twenty adults sit in tiny classroom desks while Frank stands at the whiteboard holding a Styrofoam cup of coffee he never drinks. Mara has wisely chosen a seat near the door. Danny is in the back doing paperwork.

One father asks whether Frank believes humiliation is an appropriate coaching technique.

Frank thinks about this much longer than anyone would like.

“No.”

The room relaxes slightly.

“I just don’t think that was humiliating.”

Mara closes her eyes.

A mother in the second row asks whether he has ever coached teenage girls before. Frank says no. Somebody else asks whether he has ever coached basketball before.

Frank looks at Danny.

Danny continues filling out a form.

I love that the movie gives this disaster nearly six minutes and never finds a graceful way out of it. By the time the meeting ends, three parents have checked the clock, one has left to move a car, and Frank’s coffee is still completely full.

### The free throws

In a tight game late in the season, Erin gets fouled with four seconds left.

All year, little injuries have appeared whenever pressure does. Now she is standing alone at the line with a packed student section behind the basket and nowhere particularly useful to limp.

The official hands her the ball. Erin spins it once, catches it, then glances toward the bench.

Frank raises one hand and makes a tiny motion, like he is brushing crumbs off a poker table. Before Erin can look back again, he has turned his chair toward Mara and started talking about the next possession.

The referee puts the whistle in his mouth.

## THE FINISH

By the district final, Lakewood still looks like Lakewood. The recliner is beside the bench, the scoreboard above the east basket still takes an extra second to update after free throws, and Marcus Reed is sitting in roughly the same place he has occupied all winter.

A season’s worth of pressure is now sharing one gym. Tasha is carrying the ball. Mara is calling the game. Erin is under the basket with four fouls and a strip of athletic tape coming loose above one shoe. Danny has abandoned his seat and is standing near the tunnel.

Late in the fourth quarter, Tasha turns the ball over. On the next possession, she passes up a shot she usually takes, and the noise in the gym changes as the other team comes back the other way.

Frank watches Tasha retreat on defense.

Across the floor, Marcus stands.

Tasha sees him.

On the Lakewood bench, Mara lowers the clipboard.
