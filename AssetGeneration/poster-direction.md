# Poster direction: iconic, varied, believable

The poster is the third-slide payoff, after the idea and plot have already won attention. It should still work when viewed alone. Aim for the kind of visual compression that lets someone recall a movie from one silhouette, shape, object, or improbable juxtaposition. The user's reference set included classic one-image film posters and a collection of different movie images with common design DNA: strong silhouette, controlled palette, generous negative space, an unmistakable focal point, and confidence to omit literal plot illustration. Borrow the principle, never a particular composition.

## Concept selection

Before rendering, articulate the movie's **central pressure** in one sentence. Explore several materially different visual metaphors: object, scale inversion, negative space, unexpected point of view, character relationship, environment, or typography. Pick the construction that remains legible as a small thumbnail and intriguing at full size. Ask: could this image identify this particular movie after the title is hidden? If it is only a handsome genre scene, keep searching.

For *Shared Leash*, two red leash leads meet in one collar over a single signature line. The image encodes two exes, one dog, and a decision no schedule can resolve. The ER teal/ivory field and tiny green frog toy add world and humanity without replacing the main idea. That **method** is repeatable; the leash, palette, exact Y shape, frog, and clipboard are specific to this story.

## Cohesion without monotony

The NCS family resemblance comes from sharp campaign thinking and professional restraint, not from forcing every film into cream/navy/red or a single layout. Change the medium, palette, focal scale, typography, and level of abstraction according to genre and narrative. A small domestic comedy may warrant intimate tactile objects; a creature film may warrant ruthless scale and negative space. Keep one dominant read, deliberate title placement, plausible production furniture, and a controlled number of accents.

Avoid the common failure modes: generic AI key art; every character arranged in a cast pyramid; literal multi-scene collage; ornamental details that only make sense after explanation; excessive cinematic gloss; generic floating text; identical poster composition across films. Photographic realism is one option, not a house mandate. Avoid recognizable real-actor likeness by default, and avoid faces entirely when an object or construction is stronger.

## Type and film-poster furniture

- Follow the packet's **exact title, tagline, rating, format descriptor, and footer copy**. If a field is unspecified, do not pretend it was approved. Seek editorial direction for required missing text rather than fabricate credits.
- The title and hero image should form one composition. Give the title enough scale to read on a phone; position the tagline where it deepens the visual idea. Set all exact words in a controllable type layer.
- Make the rating clear, plausible, and consistent in treatment. Use a small standardized rating/format area near the bottom if the packet specifies both. Keep it legible without making it a second headline.
- A restrained production-style footer or billing line can help sell the artifact. Use one consistent typographic treatment and baseline across a campaign, adapted for contrast. Remove verbose bottom-border prose and unnecessary descriptors. Never claim a real studio, distributor, filmmaker, shoot location, or release that the packet has not authorized.
- Film-specific typography is welcome. A recurring NCS presentation cue can quietly sign the poster but should never overpower its own fictional movie.
- The v3 carousel requires a tiny **Never Coming Soon** footer and **03 / 03** page marker on the poster. Keep them extremely restrained and separate from the fictional poster furniture so the artifact still feels convincing.
- “NOW PLAYING NOWHERE” or similar winks are optional production-specific decisions only when authorized by packet or user. “LINK IN BIO” and comment prompts do not belong on the poster.

## Final poster test

View it at phone scale and at full size. Cover the title: is there still a distinctive concept? Cover the artwork: are title, tagline, rating, and billing legible and intentionally aligned? Then view the whole carousel: does the poster feel like the payoff to the exact film described in Slide 2 rather than an unrelated attractive image?
