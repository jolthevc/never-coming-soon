# Never Coming Soon
## Editorial Quality Standard v1.1

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how to review a developed Never Coming Soon edition without flattening it into consensus prose.

The editor diagnoses.

The editor does not rewrite merely to demonstrate activity.

## 2. Review order

Review in this order:

1. production quality
2. edition strategy
3. prose quality
4. surface correctness

A sentence-level fix cannot rescue a mediocre imaginary movie.

A structural rewrite is unnecessary when the draft already works and only needs local improvement.

Fix causes before symptoms.

## 3. Production quality

Ask whether the imaginary production itself is good.

Review:

- want-to-watch factor
- story causality
- character agency
- central relationships
- escalation
- genre delivery
- signature scenes
- world when relevant
- ending
- distinctive identity
- whether the production contains any genuinely surprising, recontextualizing, or sit-up moment when the material naturally supports one

Do not require a twist or startling beat merely to satisfy a checklist. Some excellent movies accumulate rather than detonate.

If the article is beautifully written about a weak production, flag the production problem rather than rewarding the prose.

## 4. Edition quality

Ask whether the public article presents the strongest version of the production.

Review:

- opening pull
- clarity
- pace
- canonical section roles
- character presentation
- casting presentation
- plot selectivity
- signature-scene emphasis
- spoiler calibration
- proof of existence
- evidence of spectatorship
- ending appetite
- whether the genre pleasures promised by the label are actually demonstrated

The article should not read like a development memo, synopsis, database entry, Wikipedia page, or pitch deck.

## 5. Film section-role integrity

For film, inspect the six canonical public sections separately.

### THE PITCH

Should create desire for the object, not summarize the whole plot.

### THE CHARACTERS

Should make people exist through behavior, relationships, wants, habits, contradiction, and pressure.

Flag screenplay-function language such as "this character exists to" or "what makes this character interesting is."

### THE CAST

Should make the performances imaginable.

Public copy should sound as though the performance was watched, not as though the role is still being cast.

Flag language such as "needs an actor who" or generic hypothetical praise such as "would bring depth."

### THE MOVIE

Should make the production feel like an experience rather than a premise.

It should show engine, movement, collisions, texture, pressure, and enough causality to prove the movie exists without becoming a full chronological synopsis.

### THE SCENES

Should contain discrete, extractable moments.

A strong Scene often remains enjoyable even if removed from the article's narrative thread.

Flag Scenes that merely repeat material already fully staged in THE MOVIE.

### THE FINISH

Should create convergence.

It may depend on everything before it and should bring major pressures, relationships, stakes, and unresolved values into the same final movement.

It should stop before the decisive response or payoff.

Flag a Finish that simply behaves like one more standalone Scene.

## 6. Spoiler calibration

Primary rule:

**Protect whatever carries the production's major unresolved value. Give away enough genre pleasure to prove the production delivers.**

When unresolved value and genre pleasure overlap:

**Demonstrate the pleasure once in a lower-stakes situation whose outcome does not determine the whole. The demonstration is free. The stake is not.**

The article may reveal substantial events, conflict, pressure, and late-story circumstances.

It should be extremely reluctant to reveal:

- the decisive response to the central pressure
- the final emotional choice
- the meaning of the protagonist's arc
- the answer to the core mystery
- the mechanism on which the final heist or scheme depends
- the ultimate relationship resolution

Interpretive spoilers can be more damaging than event spoilers.

Flag sentences that explain what a character learns, what a scene reveals, or what the movie is really about when the reader could have discovered that meaning.

Allowed reaction:

> This is where the movie gets really funny.

Likely interpretive leak:

> This is where Frank learns that being right is not enough.

Do not become so spoiler-averse that the article feels vague, evasive, or underdeveloped.

Flag meta-withholding language such as "we do not need to know yet" or "that is where we leave them."

## 7. Proof of existence

Ask whether the production feels larger than the plot.

Look for ordinary residue such as:

- mundane institutional details
- routine background behavior
- logistical facts
- artifacts of offscreen attention
- sensory details
- things that would exist whether or not the article mentioned them

A useful test is:

**Would this detail still exist if nobody were writing an article about it?**

Do not confuse proof of existence with character detail.

Character detail should reveal a person. Proof-of-existence detail often reveals nobody important.

Flag whimsy inflation. A parade of quirky props or cute background details can make the world feel more written rather than more real.

## 8. Evidence of spectatorship

A finished edition should contain evidence that somebody watched this imaginary production and formed opinions about it.

This may be first person or not.

Look for a local reaction, preference, surprise, performance detail, joke, or moment the narrator keeps thinking about.

The reaction should not always appear in the same section or use the same sentence pattern.

A reaction may identify the pleasure. It should not explain the mechanism that produced the pleasure.

## 9. Prose quality

The prose should feel genuinely authored.

Review:

- readability
- rhythm
- sentence control
- paragraph movement
- specificity
- transitions
- humor when appropriate
- emotional sincerity
- genre voice
- narrator presence

The default house mode is clean, propulsive storytelling with selective personality.

Style should support the production rather than compete with it.

## 10. Readability

Readability does not mean short sentences.

A sentence should usually be understandable on the first read.

Longer sentences are welcome when their internal logic remains clean.

Short sentences should earn their brevity through force, surprise, comedy, emotional weight, or a genuine turn.

Do not reward bland short sentences simply because they are easy to process.

Paragraphs should feel visually inviting and narratively coherent.

## 11. Paragraph movement

A paragraph should generally contain one coherent unit of story, observation, or reaction.

A new paragraph is often useful when:

- the scene moves
- the focus shifts to another character
- a discovery changes our understanding
- a relationship turns
- a new complication begins
- the emotional beat changes

Do not impose arbitrary sentence-count limits.

One-sentence paragraphs are not forbidden. Repeated one-sentence paragraphs used as routine transitional drumbeats should be flagged.

Dialogue lines and deliberate camera-like final sequences should not be mistaken for that failure mode.

## 12. Sentence control

Prefer varied sentence structure rather than length for its own sake.

A good NCS sentence often carries a small internal turn: setup into joke, observation into implication, action into consequence, or behavior into character.

Do not use commas, semicolons, or conjunctions to keep a sentence alive after its main thought has already landed.

If a sentence contains several independent ideas or turns, split it.

Avoid both extremes:

- choppy sequences of tiny declarative sentences
- overloaded sentences that require rereading

## 13. Character test

Characters should feel like people rather than story functions.

Look for:

- behavior
- wants
- contradictions
- relationships
- recognizable habits
- choices
- consequences

Flag resume introductions, generic trauma, adjective stacks, and structural explanations of why a character exists.

Every major character should have some behavior of their own rather than being introduced entirely through what other people think or say about them.

## 14. Scene test

Signature scenes should be specific enough to picture.

Flag scenes that could be transplanted into almost any production in the genre.

A scene should usually contain a situation, someone wanting something, pressure, and a turn or sticky detail.

Dialogue should sound spoken before it sounds quotable.

Be suspicious of perfectly finished aphorisms that sound written for social sharing rather than spoken by the person in the moment.

Do not explain why the scene works after it has already worked.

## 15. Casting test

Casting should increase desire.

Flag:

- celebrity wallpaper
- generic actor praise
- actors chosen before characters are legible
- obvious chemistry problems
- repetitive NCS casting habits when another choice would be stronger
- public copy written in hypothetical development language rather than observed-performance language

## 16. Genre delivery

The edition must demonstrate the pleasures implied by the production's genre label.

A comedy should contain actual comic proof. A horror piece should create fear or dread. A heist should show some mechanism or competence. A musical should let musical spectacle exist on the page.

Do not apply one character-drama spoiler instinct to every genre.

Protect unresolved value while still showing enough of the product to make the reader want it.

## 17. No defensive writing

Flag sentences that defend the production against an imagined worse version.

Examples include:

- the movie is smart enough not to
- thankfully, this never becomes
- this does not turn into the obvious montage

The article should show the better version rather than arguing for it.

## 18. Manufactured-writing tells

Flag patterns such as:

- repeated one-sentence paragraphs
- predictable sequences of short declarative sentences
- identical paragraph shapes
- forced three-item lists
- canned transitions
- constant rhetorical questions
- repeated binary constructions
- generic concluding sentences
- overuse of parenthetical asides
- unnecessary semicolons
- prose that explains meaning immediately after showing it
- formulaic spectatorship lines
- every paragraph ending with a punchline
- every section opening with a thesis statement

Do not use em dashes in published NCS prose.

## 19. Strength preservation

Every editorial review must identify what should NOT be damaged during revision.

A strong joke, scene description, transition, character introduction, image, paragraph rhythm, performance observation, proof-of-existence detail, or structural choice should be named when worth preserving.

Revision is not automatically improvement.

## 20. Revision routing

The editor should classify the deepest problem level:

- `NONE`: ready apart from deterministic QA or trivial fixes
- `PROSE`: production and edition strategy work; revise language locally
- `EDITION`: production works, but presentation architecture needs change
- `CANON`: the imaginary production itself needs creative redevelopment

Do not route to a deeper layer than necessary.

A missing startling moment is not automatically a CANON problem. Only route deeper when the production itself is materially underpowered, not because every piece lacks a twist.

## 21. Final editorial test

Ask:

- Do I want to watch this?
- Did the article make that desire stronger?
- Did I move through it easily?
- Does the prose have personality without getting in the way?
- Can I picture the people and scenes?
- Does the world feel larger than the plot?
- Is there evidence somebody watched this imaginary production?
- Does the genre feel like itself?
- Did the article demonstrate genre pleasure without spending the major unresolved value?
- Did it react to the production without explaining its meaning?
- Are THE MOVIE, THE SCENES, and THE FINISH doing distinct jobs?
- Does any section feel generic, defensive, or manufactured?
- What should revision preserve?
- What is the deepest actual problem, if any?
