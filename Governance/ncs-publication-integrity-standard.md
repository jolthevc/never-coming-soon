# Never Coming Soon
## Publication Integrity Standard v1.2

## 1. Purpose

This standard protects the public illusion that a Never Coming Soon production exists.

Internal governance may be analytical, schematic, and explicit. Published prose may not sound like the machinery that produced it.

The audience should encounter a movie or show, not evidence of a workflow.

## 2. Core rule

**Internal editorial language must never leak into public copy.**

The writer may use internal concepts while thinking. The article must translate those concepts back into ordinary audience-side prose.

A reader should never be able to infer the checklist the system was following.

## 3. Internal terms that should not appear as editorial labels

Treat the following as internal vocabulary unless the words are genuinely required by the fictional world in their ordinary meaning:

- Contained Proof
- extractable play
- extractable scene
- unresolved value
- genre proof
- genre pleasure
- spoiler protection
- reveal policy
- reader promise
- public edition
- canon bible
- canon freeze
- development packet
- story challenge
- edition plan
- revision route
- proof of existence
- evidence of spectatorship
- section role
- episode shape
- season spine
- motif budget
- engine demonstration
- results stay protected
- pressure stacks without resolution

Never publish parenthetical process labels such as `(Contained Proof)` or lead-ins such as `Extractable play:`.

## 4. Do not narrate the editorial plan

The article should not say things like:

- the spine is simple
- the show's promise is
- this scene proves the engine
- the result stays protected
- the season escalates by
- this is the lower-stakes demonstration
- the episode exists to
- enough to point upstream without spending the last turn

The exact wording may vary. Any sentence that sounds like the writer is discussing how much story, reveal, or ending to spend is suspect.

If the structure is strong, the reader should feel it without being told the architecture.

## 5. Checklist visibility is a failure mode

A detail can be individually good and still become artificial when the article visibly collects it to satisfy a rubric.

Do not create a paragraph whose main purpose is to display six examples of world texture, six recurring objects, or six pieces of institutional residue.

Disperse texture where it naturally belongs.

The strongest proof of existence usually appears incidentally while something else is happening.

## 6. Motif budget

Recurring objects and images are useful. Over-signaling them makes the production feel designed from the outside.

A recurring object should earn each return.

Ask:

- Does the object matter differently here than it did before?
- Is this return caused by story or behavior rather than by a desire to remind the reader of a motif?
- Would the article be stronger if one recurrence disappeared?

Avoid making every section touch the same coin, clipboard, photograph, song, rule, prop, or phrase simply because it became the production's signature.

## 7. Spectator, not annotator

The narrator may react to what happened on screen.

The narrator should not annotate dramatic construction.

Good:

> I keep thinking about the way she closes the locker before answering him.

Bad:

> The locker beat pays off the season's central theme of control.

Good:

> This is the funniest five minutes in the episode.

Bad:

> This sequence functions as the show's lower-stakes proof of its comic engine.

## 8. Public character language

Public character copy describes people as they exist in the production.

Avoid language that sounds like a character dossier, writer's room note, casting brief, or arc summary.

Do not tell the reader that someone is a foil, translator, audience surrogate, emotional anchor, pressure source, or structural counterweight.

Do not summarize the whole arc in the character introduction.

## 9. Public television language

Episode capsules must sound like somebody describing episodes they watched.

Do not include internal episode-purpose labels, development notes, parenthetical governance labels, or phrases such as `this episode proves`, `the function of this episode`, or `the season needs`.

Do not use language that comments on how much of the finale or final turn the article is choosing to reveal.

Episode titles may be shown when they increase pleasure or reality. Internal planning labels may not.

## 10. Public scene language

A scene should arrive as a scene.

Do not precede it with an internal category name. Do not explain afterward which editorial obligation it satisfied.

If a scene is a Contained Proof internally, the public reader should never know that term.

## 11. Surface integrity

Before delivery, check for:

- internal process terminology
- notes-to-self language
- placeholders
- schema labels
- score commentary inside the article body
- parenthetical editorial classifications
- raw field names
- explicit spoiler-management language
- references to prompts, models, generation, automation, workflow, or tooling
- em dash characters

The Google Doc may display the NCS score above the article as delivery metadata. The article itself should not discuss its score.

## 12. Hard deterministic leak list

The orchestration layer should flag these substrings case-insensitively when they appear in public article text:

- contained proof
- extractable play
- extractable scene
- results stay protected
- the spine is simple
- pressure stacks without resolution
- unresolved value
- genre proof
- spoiler protection
- reveal policy
- reader promise
- canon bible
- canon freeze
- development packet
- story challenge
- edition plan
- revision route
- proof of existence
- evidence of spectatorship
- engine demonstration
- motif budget
- spending the last turn

Use substring matching rather than requiring a colon or exact capitalization so obvious variants do not slip through.

These are strong warnings and normally deserve focused revision before delivery.

The deterministic list is not exhaustive. The Forensic Editor must also catch semantic leakage that uses different words.

## 13. Final test

Ask:

**Could this paragraph plausibly have been written by someone who watched the production, without knowing anything about the internal NCS development system?**

If not, rewrite it from the audience side.
