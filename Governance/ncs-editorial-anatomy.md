# Never Coming Soon
## Editorial Anatomy v1.5

## 1. Governing philosophy

Never Coming Soon standardizes the reader's navigation, not the storytelling inside it.

For film:

**The Pitch makes me want the object.**  
**The Characters makes the people exist.**  
**The Cast makes the performances imaginable.**  
**The Movie demonstrates the production's primary engine in motion.**  
**The Scenes proves the movie contains moments worth remembering.**  
**The Finish brings major pressures into convergence and gets out before the production spends its decisive payoff.**

For television, the article must also make returnability legible without becoming a show bible.

The internal canon knows the whole production. The public edition is selective by design.

## 2. Cross-cutting obligations

### Proof of existence

The production should feel larger than the events required to explain the story.

Use incidental evidence that the world continues beyond the plot: mundane institutional details, logistical residue, background behavior, ordinary routines, offscreen observers, artifacts of other people's attention, and sensory facts characters barely notice.

Ask:

**Would this detail still exist if nobody were writing an article about it?**

Avoid whimsy inflation and checklist paragraphs. Disperse ordinary texture where it naturally belongs.

### Evidence of spectatorship

Every finished edition should contain evidence that somebody watched this imaginary production and formed opinions about it.

This may be a local reaction, preference, surprise, performance detail, joke, or small thing the narrator keeps thinking about.

Do not create a fixed spectatorship slot or explain why the reaction works.

### Publication integrity

Internal governance may use analytical labels. Public prose may not expose them.

Terms such as `Contained Proof`, `extractable play`, `unresolved value`, `proof of existence`, `evidence of spectatorship`, and `edition plan` belong backstage.

## 3. Spoiler doctrine

Core rule:

**Protect whatever carries the production's major unresolved value. Give away enough genre pleasure to prove the production delivers.**

When unresolved value and genre pleasure overlap, demonstrate the pleasure in a lower-stakes situation whose outcome does not determine the whole.

Do not announce withholding.

## 4. The Contained Proof

A Contained Proof is an internal lower-stakes sequence that fully demonstrates the production's primary engine or genre pleasure without spending its major unresolved value.

It demonstrates capability, not stakes, and should usually end smaller than it began.

Examples may include a survivable creature encounter, one successful con, one lower-stakes heist mechanism, one romantic sequence that proves chemistry, one musical number, or one sports sequence that proves the competitive engine.

The device is internal. Never publish `(Contained Proof)` or another annotation identifying it.

## 5. Interpretation discipline

Do not state meaning, mechanism, or consequence that the material is capable of delivering on its own.

If the material can carry the meaning or consequence, improve the material rather than appending a gloss.

## 6. Scene ownership

Every substantially treated scene or sequence should have one primary public home.

A scene may be teased in one section and fully staged in another. It should not receive two full treatments.

For film, the main duplication risk is THE MOVIE versus THE SCENES.

For television, the main duplication risk is THE SEASON versus THE EPISODES.

A full treatment generally includes several of the following:

- detailed action progression
- dialogue
- outcome
- distinctive visual or comic beats
- character reaction
- scene-specific texture

If most of those have already been spent in one section, another section should not retell the sequence with new wording.

Paraphrase is not distinct material.

The Edition Architect should assign important sequences one primary section through `scene_ownership_plan`.

## 7. Film

### Typical length

1,200 to 1,800 words. This is a center of gravity, not a quota.

### Canonical visible structure

1. Hero poster
2. Studio card + logline
3. THE PITCH
4. THE CHARACTERS
5. THE CAST
6. THE MOVIE
7. THE SCENES
8. THE FINISH
9. Final studio card

### THE PITCH

Usually 150 to 250 words.

Sell the object quickly. Introduce the premise, protagonist, world, initial collision, or problem.

The Pitch may tease later sequences but should not spend the best dramatic material the article intends to stage elsewhere.

### THE CHARACTERS

Usually 3 to 6 meaningful characters.

Describe people through behavior, relationships, wants, contradictions, habits, and pressure.

Do not explain screenplay function.

### THE CAST

Usually 3 to 6 major roles.

Character first. Actor second.

Public casting copy should sound as though we watched the performance, not as though we are still casting the role.

### THE MOVIE

Usually 300 to 500 words, with flexibility by production.

**THE MOVIE demonstrates the production's primary engine in motion. What that means depends on the genre.**

For character drama it may be relationships and pressure. For a heist, mechanism and timing. For horror, dread and survival. For action, physical problem solving. For romance, attraction, friction, choice, and consequence. For a musical, music, staging, desire, and spectacle.

The section should show what is fun, tense, moving, frightening, romantic, or otherwise distinctive about watching the production once the premise starts moving.

It should not become a chronological synopsis or beat sheet.

The Movie may contain scene fragments or dialogue, but it should not fully stage so many discrete scenes that THE SCENES has nothing different to do.

### THE SCENES

Usually 2 to 4 discrete moments.

These are extractable pieces of the movie, almost like clips somebody could show after saying, `You have to see this part.`

A strong Scene can often be removed without the reader losing the article's narrative thread.

Scenes can demonstrate genre pleasure, character chemistry, comedy, fear, mechanism, romance, spectacle, awkwardness, or another memorable experience.

Do not spend the same scene twice.

If THE MOVIE already substantially staged a sequence, choose another Scene. If a Scene receives close treatment here, keep any earlier appearance light.

### THE FINISH

Usually 80 to 175 words.

The Finish has one job no ordinary Scene has:

**convergence.**

Bring the strongest live pressures, relationships, stakes, mechanisms, and unresolved values into the same final movement, then stop before the decisive response or payoff.

Convergence is not callback density.

Do not gather every recurring prop, document, phrase, ritual, or motif into the final room merely because it appeared earlier.

Usually two or three active pressures are stronger than six callbacks.

Do not default to a recognition beat, face, approaching authority figure, or one character noticing another.

A Finish may stop on an irreversible action beginning, an object changing state, a door opening, a machine engaging, a song starting, a crowd moving, a lie becoming public, two plans colliding, or another genre-appropriate point of maximum pressure.

Avoid vague cliffhanger language that withholds without creating a specific dramatic threshold.

Do not explain that the outcome is being protected.

### Final studio card

The final studio card sits outside the fiction and may acknowledge the central joke of Never Coming Soon.

Current approved brand beat:

**NOW PLAYING NOWHERE**

**The best movies and shows that don't exist.**

## 8. Narrator position

The narrator may behave like a spectator.

The narrator should not behave like the production's marketer, screenwriter, development executive, or studio.

Avoid speaking as though we know why a scene was shot, what the budget paid for, what the studio wanted, what belongs in the trailer, or how filmmakers intended the audience to react.

## 9. Series

Detailed format governance lives in `Governance/ncs-television-editorial-standard.md`.

### Typical length

1,600 to 2,200 words.

### Default structure

1. Hero key art
2. Studio card + logline
3. THE PITCH
4. THE WORLD
5. THE CHARACTERS
6. THE CAST
7. THE SEASON
8. THE EPISODES
9. THE FINISH
10. Final studio card

### THE WORLD

Establish why this needs television rather than two hours. Show rules when they change behavior. Do not turn the section into a manual.

### THE CHARACTERS

Internal television canon may contain a large ensemble. Public copy should usually focus on 4 to 7 characters unless a broader ensemble is genuinely the attraction.

### THE CAST

Focus on the performances that most increase desire. It need not mirror the full internal cast plan.

### THE SEASON

Give the season shape without publishing its full causal map or internal labels.

Show movement rather than describing the architecture of movement.

Do not fully stage episode sequences that THE EPISODES will later own.

### THE EPISODES

THE EPISODES is a discovery section, not an inventory requirement.

A longer season may spotlight only the episodes that materially increase desire.

Every included episode needs a concrete dramatic object such as a problem, scheme, discovery, relationship collision, mystery, set piece, choice, consequence, or reversal.

Do not repeat a sequence already substantially staged in THE SEASON.

Episode capsules should sound like somebody describing episodes they watched.

### THE FINISH for television

Bring the season's major pressures into convergence while protecting the decisive finale response or resolution.

Do not narrate the fact that the result is being withheld.

## 10. Limited series

Typical length is 1,500 to 2,200 words, usually 4 to 8 episodes.

Use the series architecture when useful while preserving the contained ending and cumulative inevitability.

## 11. Character and casting order

Default sequence:

**Character first. Actor second. Story third.**

## 12. Character imagery

Character portraits are optional.

Default philosophy:

**Words create the person. Casting supplies the face. Imagery creates the world.**

## 13. Internal story bible

Generation should develop more than it publishes.

Minimum internal understanding should include the full premise, character relationships, complete story trajectory, actual ending, major scenes, tone, world logic when needed, major unresolved value worth protecting publicly, genre pleasures worth demonstrating publicly, and lower-stakes demonstration candidates when useful.

For television, include season trajectory, recurring engine, concrete episode purposes, character movement, actual finale resolution, and future direction when relevant.

## 14. Flexibility inside the structure

The main headings are standardized. The storytelling inside them is not.

Length, paragraph shape, rhythm, scene choice, amount of dialogue, narrator presence, spectacle, and emphasis should vary with the production.

A recognizable editorial product does not require formulaic writing.
