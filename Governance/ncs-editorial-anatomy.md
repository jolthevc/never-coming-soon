# Never Coming Soon
## Editorial Anatomy v1.2

## 1. Governing philosophy

Never Coming Soon standardizes the reader's navigation, not the storytelling inside it.

For film, the public edition should follow a recognizable grammar:

**The Pitch makes me want the object.**  
**The Characters makes the people exist.**  
**The Cast makes the performances imaginable.**  
**The Movie makes the production feel like an experience rather than a premise.**  
**The Scenes proves the movie contains moments worth remembering.**  
**The Finish brings everything under maximum pressure and gets out before the production pays it off.**

The internal canon knows the whole production.

The public edition is selective by design.

## 2. Two cross-cutting obligations

Every edition should satisfy two obligations that plot summary alone cannot satisfy.

### Proof of existence

The production should feel larger than the events required to explain the story.

Use incidental evidence that the world continues beyond the plot:

- mundane institutional details
- logistical residue
- background behavior
- ordinary routines
- offscreen observers
- artifacts of other people's attention
- sensory details characters barely notice

The strongest proof-of-existence details often imply people, routines, institutions, or attention outside the main narrative.

A useful test is:

**Would this detail still exist if nobody were writing an article about it?**

Do not confuse proof of existence with character detail.

Character detail is selected because it reveals a person.

Proof-of-existence detail should usually reveal nobody important. It exists because the world does.

Avoid whimsy inflation. Do not turn every production into a shelf of quirky props, crooked signs, eccentric objects, and charming background business.

### Evidence of spectatorship

The article should contain evidence that somebody has watched this imaginary production and formed opinions about it.

This can appear as:

- a local reaction
- a preference
- something unexpectedly funny
- a performance detail we particularly enjoy
- a moment that surprised us
- a small thing we keep thinking about

First person is allowed but not required.

Do not create a fixed spectatorship slot or repeated sentence pattern. The reaction should appear where it naturally belongs.

A reaction may name what we enjoyed. It should not explain the mechanism that produced the enjoyment.

Good:

> The funniest thing is that Frank never admits the recliner is ridiculous.

Bad:

> The recliner works because it externalizes Frank's refusal to take the job seriously.

The first sounds like somebody watched the movie.

The second sounds like somebody attended the story meeting.

## 3. Spoiler doctrine

The objective is appetite.

The internal production may be fully developed. The public article should not publish the experience of discovering its decisive meaning, choices, reversals, or outcomes.

Core rule:

**Protect whatever carries the production's major unresolved value. Give away enough genre pleasure to prove the production delivers.**

When unresolved value and genre pleasure are the same thing, use this tiebreak:

**Demonstrate the pleasure once in a lower-stakes situation whose outcome does not determine the whole. The demonstration is free. The stake is not.**

A horror piece can show one attack without spending the attack that decides the story.

A heist piece can show one mechanism working without explaining the mechanism on which the final job depends.

A musical can reveal a full musical pleasure without revealing what the final relationship or story resolution does with it.

Another useful distinction:

**Show the behavior, pressure, conflict, and consequences. Be extremely reluctant to explain the lesson, theme, or meaning.**

Allowed:

> This is where the movie gets really funny.

Usually not allowed:

> This is where Frank learns that being right is not enough.

The first is spectator reaction.

The second publishes the protagonist's arc.

Do not become so spoiler-averse that the article turns coy or vague.

Do not announce withholding with lines such as:

- we do not need to know yet whether it works
- whatever happens next is for the movie
- that is where we leave them

Show the pressure and stop naturally.

## 4. Film

### Typical length

1,200 to 1,800 words.

This is a center of gravity, not a quota.

Perceived reading effort matters more than raw count.

### Canonical visible structure

1. **Hero poster**
2. **Studio card + logline**
3. **THE PITCH**
4. **THE CHARACTERS**
5. **THE CAST**
6. **THE MOVIE**
7. **THE SCENES**
8. **THE FINISH**
9. **Final studio card**

The principal editorial sections above should use these visible headings.

Do not replace them with clever newsletter furniture such as "Meet the Players," "Why We Love It," or arbitrary plot-stage headings.

The personality belongs in the production and prose, not in cute section names.

### Hero poster

The primary visual object. It should communicate genre, world, tone, title, and ambition quickly enough to travel on social by itself.

### Studio card + logline

Typical fields:

- FILM
- genre
- runtime
- rating when useful
- setting or era when useful
- one-sentence logline

### THE PITCH

Usually 150 to 250 words.

Sell the object quickly.

Introduce the premise, protagonist, world, initial collision, or problem. By the end, the reader should want the movie.

The Pitch is not The Plot. Its job is desire.

### THE CHARACTERS

Usually 3 to 6 meaningful characters.

Characters appear before formal casting.

Describe people through behavior, relationships, wants, contradictions, habits, and pressure.

Do not explain screenplay function.

Avoid language such as:

- what makes this character interesting is
- this character exists to
- this character prevents the movie from
- this character becomes the protagonist's translator

**Characters tells me who these people are. The Movie tells me what happens when they collide.**

### THE CAST

Usually 3 to 6 major roles.

Character first. Actor second.

Public casting copy should sound as though we watched the performance, not as though we are still casting the role.

Prefer:

> Walton Goggins is Frank Mercer, and about five minutes in the whole thing makes sense.

Avoid:

> Frank needs an actor who can balance charm and danger.

The latter is useful internal casting logic but wrong public voice.

### THE MOVIE

Usually 300 to 500 words, with flexibility by production.

The Movie should make the reader feel the experience, engine, movement, collisions, texture, and escalation of the production.

It should not become a chronological synopsis or beat sheet.

Give enough causal movement to prove a full movie exists. Do not explain the complete emotional machinery.

A useful question is:

**What is it like to watch this movie once the premise begins moving?**

The Movie may contain scene fragments or dialogue, but it should not fully stage so many discrete scenes that THE SCENES has nothing different to do.

### THE SCENES

Usually 2 to 4 discrete moments.

These are extractable pieces of the movie, almost like clips somebody could show after saying, "You have to see this part."

They do not have to be the most plot-important scenes.

A strong Scene can often be removed without the reader losing the thread of the article.

That is a feature, not a bug.

Scenes can demonstrate genre pleasure, character chemistry, comedy, fear, mechanism, romance, spectacle, awkwardness, or another memorable experience.

Do not spend the same scene twice. If a scene receives close treatment here, keep any earlier reference to it light.

### THE FINISH

Usually 80 to 175 words.

The Finish has one job no ordinary Scene has:

**convergence.**

A Scene should be extractable. The Finish may depend on everything the reader has already absorbed.

Bring the major pressures, relationships, stakes, and unresolved values into the same final movement.

Then stop before the decisive response or payoff.

The Finish establishes final pressure. It does not explain what the pressure ultimately produces.

### Final studio card

The final studio card sits outside the fiction and may deliberately acknowledge the central joke of Never Coming Soon.

It should do so confidently, not apologetically.

Taglines, fictional release language, and concise brand treatment are welcome.

A current approved brand beat is:

**NOW PLAYING NOWHERE**

followed by:

**The best movies and shows that don't exist.**

## 5. Series

### Typical length

1,700 to 2,300 words.

Longer because episodic proof is part of the entertainment.

### Current default structure

1. Hero key art
2. Studio card + logline
3. **THE PITCH**
4. **THE WORLD**
5. **THE CHARACTERS**
6. **THE CAST**
7. **THE SEASON**
8. **THE EPISODES**
9. **THE FINISH**
10. Final studio card

This is the current default, not yet a gold-validated television template. Television requires separate calibration because episode guides create different spoiler and pacing problems from film.

### THE WORLD

Establish why this is television rather than two hours.

What institution, relationship network, profession, mystery, competition, family, place, system, or recurring problem keeps generating episodes?

### THE SEASON

Give the season shape without publishing its full causal map or finale meaning.

### THE EPISODES

Usually 8 to 10 episodes.

Typical capsule length is 70 to 100 words, with flexibility.

Every episode needs a real dramatic object:

- a problem
- a scheme
- a discovery
- a relationship collision
- a mystery
- a set piece
- a choice
- a consequence
- a reversal

Avoid vague descriptions such as "pressure mounts" or "relationships are tested" unless attached to a concrete episode premise.

Episode capsules should make the season feel real without summarizing the complete season into eight consecutive spoilers.

### THE FINISH for television

Bring the season's major pressures into convergence while protecting the decisive finale response or resolution.

The exact television treatment remains subject to future gold-example calibration.

## 6. Limited series

Typical length is 1,600 to 2,300 words.

Usually 6 to 8 episodes.

Use the current series architecture while making the contained destination feel inevitable without publishing the contained ending.

## 7. Character and casting order

Default sequence:

**Character first. Actor second. Story third.**

The reader meets the person, receives the face, then watches that person move through the production.

## 8. Character imagery

Character portraits are optional.

Default philosophy:

**Words create the person. Casting supplies the face. Imagery creates the world.**

Use explicit character imagery only when it materially improves the production.

## 9. Internal story bible

Generation should develop more than it publishes.

Minimum internal understanding should include:

- full premise
- character relationships
- complete story trajectory
- actual ending
- major scenes
- tone
- world logic when needed
- unresolved value worth protecting publicly
- genre pleasures worth demonstrating publicly

For television, include season trajectory, episode purposes, character arcs, actual finale resolution, and future direction when relevant.

## 10. Flexibility inside the structure

The main Film headings are standardized.

The storytelling inside them is not.

Length, paragraph shape, rhythm, scene choice, amount of dialogue, narrator presence, and emphasis should vary with the production.

Internal subheads should be rare outside THE SCENES. Scene titles are natural subheads because each Scene is a discrete object.

Do not create top-level plot-stage headings such as "The First Few Weeks" or "The Thing Frank Gets Wrong." Put that material inside THE MOVIE.

A recognizable editorial product does not require formulaic writing.
