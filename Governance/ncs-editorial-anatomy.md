# Never Coming Soon
## Editorial Anatomy v1.0

## 1. Governing principle

The internal structure should be consistent enough to protect quality.

The published experience should never feel templated.

The system knows what ingredients belong in a strong edition. The reader simply experiences a great pitch.

## 2. Film

### Typical length

1,200 to 1,800 words.

This is a center of gravity, not a quota.

Perceived reading effort matters more than raw count.

### Canonical ingredients

1. **Hero poster**
2. **Studio card and logline**
3. **The pitch**
4. **The characters**
5. **Dream cast**
6. **The movie**
7. **The scenes we can't stop thinking about**
8. **The home stretch**
9. **Final studio card**

### Hero poster

The primary visual object. It should communicate genre, world, tone, title, and ambition quickly enough to travel on social by itself.

### Studio card

Typical fields:

- FILM
- genre
- setting
- runtime
- rating when useful
- era when useful
- one-sentence logline

### The pitch

Usually 150 to 220 words.

The opening conversation. Introduce the premise, protagonist, world, or problem quickly. By the end, the reader should be in.

### The characters

Usually 3 to 6 characters.

Characters appear before formal casting.

Each receives a concise portrait built from desire, behavior, contradiction, relationships, and what makes them worth watching.

### Dream cast

Usually 3 to 6 major roles.

Actor follows character.

A casting explanation should illuminate the fit. It should not become celebrity wallpaper.

### The movie

Usually 450 to 650 words.

Tell enough of the story that the reader understands the shape of the experience. Move quickly through connective material and slow down where the production becomes distinctive.

Do not write an exhaustive beat sheet.

### Signature scenes

Usually 2 to 4 moments.

These are the scenes that make the production tangible. They do not have to be the most plot-important scenes.

### The home stretch

Usually 80 to 150 words.

Bring the reader toward the climax. Make the remaining stakes clear. Protect the ultimate payoff by default.

### Final studio card

A closing brand artifact. It may contain a tagline, fictional release language, a line from the production, tiny credit treatment, or another final flourish.

The CTA should remind the reader that they cannot watch it, but they can read the whole thing or subscribe for more.

## 3. Series

### Typical length

1,700 to 2,300 words.

Longer because the episode guide is part of the entertainment.

### Canonical ingredients

1. Hero key art
2. Studio card and logline
3. The pitch
4. The world and recurring engine
5. The characters
6. Dream cast
7. Season One
8. Episode guide
9. Where Season One leaves us
10. Final studio card

### World and recurring engine

The reader should understand why this needs television rather than two hours.

What institution, relationship, profession, mystery, competition, family, place, or system keeps generating story?

### Episode guide

Usually 8 to 10 episodes.

Typical capsule length is 70 to 100 words, with flexibility.

Every episode needs a real dramatic object:

- a problem
- a scheme
- a discovery
- a relationship collision
- a mystery
- a set piece
- a choice
- a consequence
- a reversal

Avoid vague descriptions such as "pressure mounts" or "relationships are tested" unless attached to a concrete episode premise.

Later episodes can reveal increasingly consequential situations while protecting the final resolution.

## 4. Limited series

Typical length is 1,600 to 2,300 words.

Usually 6 to 8 episodes.

Follow the series architecture, but the full story should clearly build toward a contained ending.

## 5. Character and casting order

Default sequence:

**Character first. Actor second. Story third.**

The reader meets the person, receives the face, then watches that person move through the production.

## 6. Character imagery

Character portraits are optional.

Default philosophy:

**Words create the person. Casting supplies the face. Imagery creates the world.**

Use explicit character imagery only when it materially improves the production.

## 7. Spoiler policy

Never Coming Soon is neither spoiler-free nor a plot-summary publication.

The objective is appetite.

We can reveal setup, meaningful escalation, selected scenes, character turns, and late-story circumstances.

We usually protect the decisive final choice, final emotional payoff, and answer to the central mystery.

## 8. Internal story bible

Generation should develop more than it publishes.

Minimum internal understanding should include:

- full premise
- character relationships
- complete story trajectory
- actual ending
- major scenes
- tone
- world logic when needed

For television, include season trajectory, episode purposes, character arcs, and possible future direction when relevant.

## 9. Section flexibility

These are required ingredients, not mandatory visible headings.

The public edition should breathe and adapt to the production.
