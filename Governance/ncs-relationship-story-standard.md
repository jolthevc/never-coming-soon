# Never Coming Soon
## Relationship Story Standard v1.0

## 1. Purpose

This standard governs productions whose central pleasure depends materially on romance, romantic comedy, second-chance love, or another two-person relationship engine.

It exists because a clever mechanism can create proximity without creating chemistry.

The central test is:

**Do we want these two specific people together, apart from the device that keeps putting them in the same room?**

## 2. Chemistry must appear in behavior

Do not rely on narration, casting, shared history, or premise mechanics to tell us two people have chemistry.

Create actual interaction that demonstrates some combination of:

- conversational rhythm that belongs specifically to the pair
- mutual amusement
- desire
- private shorthand
- enjoyable friction
- attention to details other people miss
- vulnerability that changes how one person sees the other
- competence or generosity that becomes attractive in motion
- a moment where being together is plainly more pleasurable than merely being near each other

At least one substantial romantic sequence should still work if the external premise mechanism were temporarily removed.

The dog, wedding, job, trip, competition, fake relationship, family crisis, or other device may create the encounter. The people themselves must make the romance worth watching.

## 3. Romantic comedy must be both things

A romantic comedy needs actual romantic pleasure and actual comedy.

Situational comedy around logistics, family, work, pets, travel, mistaken identity, or social pressure is useful, but it cannot fully substitute for the central pair being funny together.

Ask:

- Do they make each other laugh?
- Do they surprise one another?
- Is there dialogue or behavior that only this pair could produce?
- Are there moments where the comedy itself increases attraction or intimacy?

If the answer is no, the production may be a relationship drama with comic circumstances rather than a satisfying romantic comedy.

## 4. Second-chance romance requires first-breakup logic

A second-chance romance needs an actual reason the first relationship ended.

`They drifted apart`, `timing was bad`, or `they broke up kindly` may establish tone, but they do not by themselves create a credible romantic obstacle.

Internally know:

- why the relationship ended
- why separation felt correct at the time
- what each person contributed to the failure or incompatibility
- what has changed, or would have to change, before reunion could be more than nostalgia
- what choosing each other now would cost or require

The public article does not need a speech explaining the breakup. The production does need to make the logic legible through behavior and choices.

If the pair is obviously excellent together and the breakup reason remains vague, the story creates the wrong question: `Why are these people still apart?`

## 5. Bilateral stakes

In a true two-hander, both people need lives that continue outside the relationship.

Do not give one lead a vivid career, home, friendship network, new relationship, ambition, geographic future, or meaningful sacrifice while the other mainly waits to be chosen.

The stakes do not need to be equal in size. They should be independently real.

A satisfying reunion should require both people to choose, adjust, risk, or relinquish something.

A satisfying separation should also feel like two lives moving forward rather than one person winning and the other being left behind.

## 6. New partners deserve dignity

A new partner should not exist only to be obviously wrong, cruel, boring, or disposable.

When a new partner is part of the story, give that person enough independent value to make the central choice harder and more revealing.

Do not ask the audience to dislike a decent person merely to clear the runway for the preferred couple.

A good new partner can sharpen the story by revealing what the protagonist actually wants, what the old relationship lacked, or what still has not changed.

## 7. Relationship pressure should create decisions

External mechanisms are useful when they force choices rather than merely prolong contact.

Ask:

- What does this situation make one person ask of the other?
- What boundary becomes harder to maintain?
- What new future becomes possible or impossible?
- What does one person risk by staying available?
- What does one person lose by choosing the relationship?

Repeated proximity without changing decisions becomes episodic stalling.

## 8. Public-edition proof

When romance is a major genre promise, the public edition should include at least one meaningful interaction that proves chemistry without spending the final romantic choice.

Do not let the article prove only that the premise is clever, the logistics are realistic, or the actors are well cast.

The reader should encounter at least one moment that makes the relationship itself desirable.

## 9. Final relationship test

Before canon freeze, ask:

- Can we feel chemistry rather than merely understand compatibility?
- Can we identify why these two people specifically are drawn to each other?
- If this is a second-chance story, is the original breakup credible and specific?
- What would genuinely need to change before reunion could work?
- Do both leads have lives and stakes outside the relationship?
- Does each lead make consequential choices?
- Are new partners treated as people rather than obstacles?
- Does the genre deliver actual romantic pleasure on screen?

If a material answer is no, keep developing.
