# Never Coming Soon
## Story Development Standard v1.0

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how Never Coming Soon turns a promising Development Packet into an imagined movie or television production that genuinely deserves to exist.

Generation is not an exercise in filling fields. It is creative development.

The central question is simple:

**If this production were real, would we actually want to watch it?**

## 2. Development authority

The Development Packet explains why Ideation cared.

It does not lock the production.

Generation may change title, format, genre emphasis, characters, relationships, setting, era, plot mechanics, scenes, climax, ending, episode structure, and any other provisional choice.

The default obligation is:

**Preserve or improve the creative kernel. Treat every other element as provisional.**

Do not protect weak choices out of loyalty to the first version.

## 3. The production must live beyond the logline

A strong production should create scenes, choices, relationships, reversals, complications, and emotional movement after the premise is understood.

The logline is the door, not the house.

Warning signs:

- the entire idea is one twist
- the premise is more interesting than any scene it produces
- characters exist mainly to explain the hook
- escalation means only making the stakes larger
- the story keeps adding mechanisms because the human material is thin

Prefer concepts with high creative yield from a clean dramatic engine.

## 4. Story pressure

A story needs pressure that forces people to choose, reveal themselves, change tactics, collide, or pay a price.

Pressure can come from competition, desire, obligation, family, romance, time, danger, pride, money, secrecy, ambition, grief, responsibility, social expectation, physical constraint, or another source appropriate to the genre.

Pressure should not feel bolted on merely to make the plot move.

The best pressure grows naturally from what the characters want and the situation they are in.

## 5. Causality

Important events should usually create later events.

Avoid a story that feels like a sequence of unrelated incidents.

Ask:

- What changes because this happened?
- Who reacts?
- What new problem or possibility does that reaction create?
- What choice becomes harder?
- What earlier behavior now has a consequence?

Coincidence may begin a story. It should not solve one.

## 6. Characters act

Characters should make decisions that shape the production.

A protagonist who only receives information, survives events, or waits for plot is usually weak.

Strong characters:

- want something
- make choices
- have habits and behaviors
- contain contradictions
- affect other people
- can be wrong in interesting ways
- change tactics when circumstances change

Do not confuse biography with character.

Do not use trauma as the default source of depth.

A character can be fascinating because of pride, generosity, pettiness, competence, jealousy, optimism, laziness, loyalty, appetite, fear, vanity, curiosity, humor, faith, stubbornness, or a hundred other human qualities.

## 7. Relationships create story

The strongest productions often have a relationship at the center that keeps producing material.

A good relationship contains motion.

People may need one another while resenting it. They may love each other while wanting incompatible things. They may misunderstand each other, compete, protect, manipulate, admire, disappoint, forgive, or slowly become important to one another.

Do not reduce relationships to labels such as mentor, rival, spouse, sibling, or partner.

Ask what these two specific people do to one another.

## 8. Escalation

Escalation is not simply bigger stakes.

A story can escalate because:

- a choice closes off an easy exit
- success creates a more difficult problem
- a relationship becomes more important
- somebody learns something inconvenient
- a private problem becomes public
- a character doubles down
- a lie becomes harder to maintain
- the cost of getting what someone wants changes
- two previously separate pressures collide

Escalation should deepen the experience, not merely increase volume.

## 9. Turns and reversals

A strong turn changes the reader's or character's understanding of what comes next.

Good turns often emerge from prior character behavior rather than arbitrary surprise.

A reversal can come from a decision, discovery, betrayal, success, failure, arrival, departure, change in allegiance, new obligation, or unexpected consequence.

Do not worship twists.

Predictable but emotionally inevitable can be better than surprising but arbitrary.

## 10. Signature scenes

Every developed production should contain scenes that make us wish it existed.

Signature scenes need not be the most plot-important moments.

They may be:

- hilarious
- romantic
- frightening
- tense
- visually unforgettable
- emotionally painful
- triumphant
- awkward
- strange in a way that belongs specifically to this production

A good signature scene has enough situation, behavior, tension, and detail that someone can begin directing it in their head.

Avoid generic scene labels such as "the big confrontation" or "the emotional speech."

## 11. Genre delivery

A production should sincerely deliver the pleasure of its genre.

Comedy must produce comedy.

Horror must create fear, dread, shock, unease, or another coherent horror experience.

Romance must care about chemistry, desire, vulnerability, and emotional consequence.

Thrillers need pressure and forward motion.

Action needs set pieces and physical stakes that emerge from character and situation.

Sports stories must care about the competition itself, not treat the sport as wallpaper.

Mysteries need questions worth following and answers worth receiving.

Family entertainment should respect children and adults rather than flatten either audience.

Drama does not mean humorless suffering.

Prestige is not a genre.

## 12. Tone

Tone should emerge from the production rather than from a universal NCS template.

A broad comedy may be ridiculous.

A romance can be sincere.

A family movie can be warm.

A thriller can be clean and propulsive.

A tragedy can breathe.

Do not add cynicism to prove sophistication.

Do not add jokes to protect sincere moments.

## 13. World

The world matters when it creates behavior, pressure, texture, or story.

A profession, town, sport, institution, era, industry, family system, school, political environment, or subculture should affect what people can do and what matters to them.

Avoid decorative specificity.

Research should produce usable dramatic detail rather than trivia.

## 14. Simplicity and complexity

Complexity must earn its place.

Before adding another timeline, secret, villain, conspiracy, institution, mythology layer, murder, betrayal, or surprise reveal, ask whether the existing human engine has been fully exploited.

A cleaner story with richer people is often stronger than a complicated story with thin people.

Do not mistake confusion for sophistication.

## 15. Film standard

A film needs a complete dramatic experience.

Internally, Generation should know:

- the setup
- what changes the situation
- what the protagonist begins doing about it
- how choices and consequences escalate
- the major turn or reorientation
- the late-story pressure
- the climactic situation
- the actual ending
- the emotional resolution

This is not a demand for a rigid screenplay formula.

The production should nevertheless feel causal, shaped, and complete.

The ending should not merely stop the plot. It should answer or transform something the movie made us care about.

## 16. Series standard

A series needs more than an excellent pilot premise.

Generation must identify a recurring engine that can keep creating episodes without repeating the same story.

The engine may come from:

- a workplace
- a family system
- a competition
- a community
- a mission structure
- a relationship network
- a profession
- an institution
- an investigation framework
- an ongoing objective with changing obstacles

Season One also needs shape.

Internally know:

- the season spine
- what changes across the season
- major character movement
- several concrete episode stories
- the finale situation
- the actual Season One resolution
- why another season could still exist, when relevant

Do not stretch a film into eight episodes.

## 17. Limited series standard

A limited series should justify multiple chapters while moving toward a contained ending.

It needs both episodic progression and cumulative inevitability.

If the material would clearly be stronger as a two-hour film, change the format.

## 18. Ending standard

An ending should feel earned by the production that came before it.

Good endings may be happy, sad, ambiguous, triumphant, quiet, devastating, funny, romantic, or unresolved in selected ways.

Ask:

- Did important choices matter?
- Did the central relationship move?
- Did the ending pay off the genre promise?
- Did the production become more specific as it approached the end rather than more generic?
- Is the final emotional effect the right one?

Do not save a weak story with a clever final twist.

## 19. Development rescue

Generation should rescue before rejecting.

If the first version has a weak second act, replace the second act.

If the protagonist is wrong, change the protagonist.

If the format is wrong, change the format.

If the ending is weak, rebuild the ending.

If the packet contains one excellent relationship inside a mediocre premise, consider rebuilding around the relationship.

Development effort should be spent making the production better, not defending previous decisions.

## 20. Canon readiness test

Before internal canon is frozen, ask:

- Would we genuinely watch this?
- Do the characters make choices?
- Is there at least one relationship with real movement?
- Does the story generate specific scenes?
- Does escalation come from the dramatic engine rather than arbitrary complication?
- Does the genre sincerely deliver?
- Does the ending feel earned?
- Does the production have its own identity?
- Is the world doing useful work?
- Can we imagine more than the logline?

If the answer is no, keep developing.
